I spend my working life thinking about risk — what could go wrong with a
program, and what the cost is if it does. I didn't apply that same
discipline to my own finances for longer than I'd like to admit, because
investing is the fun, visible part of personal finance and insurance is the
part that just sits there costing you money until, hopefully, it never
needs to.

That's exactly backwards, and it's the first thing I'd fix for anyone
starting out.

## Protection before growth, every time

A financial plan without adequate insurance isn't a plan with one weak
point — it's a plan with no floor. A market downturn costs you paper
returns; a serious illness or the loss of an income earner without cover
in place can undo a decade of disciplined saving in a single event. The
sequencing matters: term insurance and health insurance are what make it
safe to take on other financial goals in the first place, not something
to get around to after the portfolio's in good shape.

## Term insurance: buy the boring one

A term plan is pure protection — a large payout to your family if you die
within the policy term, and nothing back if you don't. That "nothing back"
is exactly why it's cheap: you're not funding an investment component
alongside the cover, you're just buying the cover. Coverage of roughly
10–15x your annual income is a common starting rule of thumb, adjusted for
outstanding loans and dependents' future needs.

The mistake I see most often is buying an endowment or ULIP plan instead,
because it "gives something back." Those products bundle insurance with a
weak investment component, and you generally end up under-insured and
under-invested at the same time — worse at both jobs than doing them
separately would have been. If you want to invest, invest. If you want
insurance, buy term.

## Health insurance: the one that erodes wealth silently

Term insurance protects your family if you're gone. Health insurance
protects your savings while you're very much still here. A serious medical
event without adequate cover doesn't just cost the treatment — it can mean
liquidating investments at a bad time, or worse, going into debt for it.
This is the risk that quietly undoes financial plans that otherwise looked
solid on paper, because it doesn't announce itself the way a market crash
does.

A few things I've found worth checking specifically: whether the policy
covers pre-existing conditions after a reasonable waiting period, whether
it's a individual or family floater plan and whether that fits your actual
household, and what the room-rent sub-limits are — a policy that looks
generous on the cover amount can still leave you paying out of pocket if
the room-rent cap doesn't match real hospital costs in your city.

## Then, and only then, the investments

None of this is an argument against investing — it's an argument about
order. Once term and health cover are in place, every rupee that goes into
equity, debt, or anything else is money you can actually afford to have
at risk, because the things that would otherwise force you to break that
plan early are already covered. That sequencing is, in my experience, the
single highest-leverage decision in a financial plan, and the one people
skip most often because it doesn't feel like progress the way a growing
portfolio does.

This isn't personalized advice — insurance needs vary a lot by income,
dependents, and existing cover, and it's worth working through the numbers
for your own situation rather than applying a rule of thumb blindly. But
the ordering — protection, then growth — is one I'd stand behind for
almost anyone.
